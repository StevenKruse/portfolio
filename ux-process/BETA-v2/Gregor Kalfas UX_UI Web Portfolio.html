My Process

User Experience Design is that part of my identity where I actively engage each of my intuitive, analytical, critical and creative mindsets.

It's not about right-brain, left-brain thinking. It's about taking a holistic approach to the projects I work on — grounding myself in understanding, gaining perspective, embracing the collaborative process, finding ways to test multiple ideas and iterating — to arrive at the most effective and practicable solutions.

While no two projects are alike, the way I think about and approach user experience design is rather straightforward, and can be summarized below, with examples of what I produce.

Research to understand

I rely on my business and consumer marketing acumen to take in market research, define business goals, and learn about the brand, the product and the stakeholders.

  • Content Audits
  • Competetive Analyses
  • Heuristic Evaluations
  • Stakeholder Interviews
Listen to learn

I structure interviews to elicit storytelling, and I document users' thoughts, feelings and actions when conducting contextual inquiries and user testing.

  • Questionnaires
  • User Interviews
  • Usability Evaluation
  • User Stories
Synthesize to define

Pulling together what I've learned, I model representative experiences, tasks and mindsets to articulate the users' goals, and define and frame the problem.

  • Personas
  • Empathy Maps
  • Mind Maps
  • Experience Maps
  • User Needs Analysis
  • User Journeys
Generate concepts

Collaboration is essential to any ideation process, whether through team brainstorming or engaging in participatory design with stakeholders and customers.

  • Structured Brainstorming
  • Ideation Workshops
  • Concept Organization
  • Problem Framing
  • Sketching
  • Storyboards
Visualize the interaction

Visualizing and diagramming the user interaction is something I do in tandem with prototype development, as it is informed by user feedback on user testing and serves to guide later iterations.

  • Use Cases
  • Wireframes
  • User Flow Diagrams
  • Site Maps
Communicate the idea

I take concepts from sketches and wireframes into visual/tangible MVPs and develop low, medium and high fidelity prototypes at multiple stages to get perspective and test with users.

  • Rapid Prototyping
  • Paper Prototypes
  • HTML Prototypes
  • Visual Mockups
Test the prototype

Getting timely and useful feedback on prototyped ideas in a structured or informal setting is crucial to the iterative process.

  • Usability Testing
  • Card Sorting
  • A/B Testing
  • Guerilla Testing
Refine and repeat

The feedback from the testing is what drives the iterative process, giving me essential insight into refining the prototype, rethinking the interaction, even generating more concepts.

About Gregor

I find creative and effective user-centered design solutions.

I draw upon a diverse background, one with roots in marketing— in corporate, agency and startup environments— and which gave me experience in consumer brand communication, managing business objectives, conducting and interpreting market research, cross-team collaboration, and more.

My professional journey continued as I nurtured my creative side through graphic and web design, and mastered front-end development, which extended my ability to develop more effective solutions for my clients. I find I enjoy the challenge of achieving visual unity in a composition as much as I do finding a creative solution using JavaScript or PHP.

I’m in my element when I’m creating, but I understand that creativity without context lacks purpose.

My identity as a user experience (UX) designer is grounded in a capacity for cognitive empathy, a deep interest in how people connect through verbal and visual communication— and a passion for languages and cultures, enriched by years of living abroad.

Now certified in Experiential and Human-Centered Design, and having trained in the various stages of user experience and user interaction design, I feel my multifacted background and skillset has given me a deeper understanding of — and unique perspective into — the UX/UI design process. Upstream

  • User Experience Design Project
  • Service Design
  • User Interaction Design

Upstream was the result of a personal project I developed through the multiple phases of user experience design, from discovery and ideation through storyboarding and personas to information architecture and interaction design.

The real-world problem I chose for the basis of my project was the practice (and perceived nuisance) of sidewalk canvassing, or "clipboard" fundraising.

The Challenge

Clipboard fundraising is a practice met with disdain by many individuals on the street, yet which still provides a significant enough return for some organizations to continue the practice. I intended to find an alternative means of direct-appeal interaction which was both less obtrusive for the individual, yet still provided positive fundraising returns for the organization.

The Approach

Discovery

After conducting some background research on fundraising and how direct-appeal engagement works, I prepared questionnaires and conducted stakeholder interviews, with individuals on the street, with the "clipboarders" themselves, and with a staff member of a fundraising organization.

Observations and Insights

I recruited some UX colleagues to help me organize the thought processes involved in a typical street canvassing encounter.

My findings led me to conclude that there was an overwhelming disdain for the obtrusive/disruptive nature of "clipboarders," asking for one's time, and petitioning for money or signatures, through a confrontational and emotional appeal. At the same time, the direct appeal approach continues to prove effective both in terms of being able to communicate effectively and presenting an immediate call-to-action.

Synthesis

I used the results of my findings to develop a mind map on sidewalk canvassing, personas, a journey map, storyboards, and comparative audits (on alternative methods of fundraising appeals).

This led to my formulation of the question:

How might we improve the experience of direct-appeal engagement for individuals while maintaining a positive return on fundraising and for the organization?

My Solution

Keeping in mind the benefits for stakeholders of direct appeal fundraising, I held brainstorming sessions which eventually led to the idea for a service which rewards individuals for donations/information through an online interface.

My focus was on using direct engagement to incentivize donation through low-cost, high-value rewards in the form of access to premium streaming content through partnerships with content providers.

Service Design

The result was Upstream, which enables users to earn reward points on donations they make, which they can apply to streaming content accounts. Upstream relies on direct engagement (or other calls-to-action) to encourage donation on one's own time via scan codes and links, and provides access to learn more about the causes to which they're giving.

Interaction Design

After creating several initial wireframes, I developed a site map and a user flow diagram to lay out potential user interactions with the site/app, covering user login, account sign up and multiple methods of code redemption.

Visual Design

I designed a print card that contains the QR code and information both about the organization and about earning rewards through Upstream. The card serves as a reminder for those who aren't prepared to participate right away, while at the same time can be passed on to others to benefit from the rewards incentivization.

I also created a high-fidelity mockup representing the mobile screen interaction with Upstream.

Though structured as a personal project, I implemented user testing throughout each stage of the process. The case study slide deck for the project (pdf) can be accessed here.

Farm Direct

  • Human-Centered Design Challenge
  • Team Project
  • Service Design
  • Rapid Prototyping
  • User Testing

Farm Direct was the result of a team collaboration project I completed with two colleagues (Angela Riewe and Gabriel Lujan) as part of the Course for Human-Centered Design led by IDEO.org and Acumen.

My teammates and I took a poverty-related design challenge through the stages of inspiration, ideation, and implementation to create prototype of our solution, which we were able to test.

The Challenge

The Course for Human-Centered Design focused on applying the mindset and methods in human-centered design to create innovative and effective solutions to create social change.

For our team challenge, the topic we decided to take on was one of providing healthier food options to people in need. It was left to our team to interpret ("provide," "healthy food" and "people in need") and to define the scope of the problem, as well as how we might go about addressing it.

After some discussion, we chose to focus on the "food desert" in the San Francisco neighborhood of Bayview-Hunter's Point ("BHP"), where low socioeconomic development has resulted in very limited accessibility to fresh food and produce, while access to junk and fast food options remains abundant.

The Approach

Discovery

Our team's initial phase began with a discussion of our assumptions and challenging what we believed we knew about the topic. Each of us then set about learning all we could on food desserts, gathering information on the BHP neighborhood and identifying types of people we could learn from, which we then used to craft an interview guide.

We scheduled a full day in BHP, where met and spoke with local residents, shoppers at a low-end supermarket, a corner grocery owner and community organizers.

We also identified and met with sources of analogous inspiration not in BHP — specifically, to a food truck owner and to vendors at a farmers' market — to add perspective and learn from those outside of our immediate focus.

Observations and Insights

Our team then re-grouped to synthesize what we had learned, and conduct a brainstorming session in which we identified themes, refined our insights, and crafted both frameworks for the problem and ideas for potential solutions. This led us to frame our challenge as:

"How might we make affordable delivery of fresh produce accessible to people living in Bayview-Hunter's Point?"

Our Solution

Our proposed solution was first a service design concept, "Farm Direct": a mobile farmers' market, supplied with lower cost fresh produce surplus from farm vendors at markets outside of BHP. The produce would be collected from various farmers' markets at the end of market, stored centrally overnight, and distributed the next day at scheduled locations and times throughout the BHP neighborhood.

With the service concept defined, we then created storyboards to helped us visualize the process and how users might interact with the service.

Prototype Development and User Testing

The final stage of our design challenge was to create some kind of usable prototype which would allow us both to demonstrate the service, and get some user feedback.

Together we conceptualized a mobile app/site that would make the service accessible to residents, considered what content would be relevant and useful, and worked on the information architecture.

I created the Farm Direct app prototype, using Adobe's Experience Design software. The usable prototype can be accessed here.

In addition to testing the prototype with local residents to get their feedback, we created online feedback forms that would allow us to extend our user testing capabilities.

MtnStuff.com

  • UX/UI and Site Design
  • Wordpress Integration
  • Front-end Development
  • PHP Scripting

I was asked by outdoor clothing and equipment distributor Great Plains Mountain Stuff ("MtnStuff") of Denver, CO, to redesign their website.

I developed a user interface and process which allowed MtnStuff to more effectively communicate with and serve their retailers, while capturing the outdoor/active lifestyle of the lines they rep.

Site URL: www.mtnstuff.com

The Challenge

For years, MtnStuff contended with a bare bones, basic website that did little more than give background on the company, show the logos of their vendors, and a map of the territory they covered.

Although the primary concern of the company's president was a "better looking site" to improve MtnStuff's image, it soon became clear that the site had multiple stakeholders in the company, each wanting to ensure the site provided specific functionality for its users.

The Approach

Discovery

I performed site audits of similar companies, and presented the client with a brief comparative analysis which— through interviews with the various stakeholders and clients— helped me develop an overall needs assessment for the site.

This allowed me to frame the problem more concisively:

"How might we develop a more enticing online presence which better facilitates interaction between MtnStuff and its retailers?"

Defining Requirements

From the above, I got buy-in for key site objectives— specifically, that the site:

  • Reflect MtnStuff's outdoor-active lifestyle image
  • Support the brands MtnStuff represents
  • Provide password-protected pages for retailers to access brand-specific forms/assets
  • Integrate with a CMS and accessible user interface to update content and upload documents
  • Include testimonials/awards and image gallery
  • Integrate with social media accounts and a blog
  • Be scalable

My Solution

Due to the accessibility of its user-interface and ease of integration with blog posts, I chose to use the Wordpress CMS as my platform, which in turn enabled me to implement customized UI features and site-specific post types.

Information Architecture

Based on site requirements and desire for simplicity I decided on a hybrid approach to site structure, one which had the feel of a single-page site, while providing access to user-specific and password-protected pages.

I did a preliminary set of wireframes to get a better idea of how to layout the site through responsive design —all of my sites use RWD— and help me select a theme which best serves that layout. The theme I used was "Sydney" by aThemes.

Site Design and Layout

Although the theme provided basic structure, as a front-end developer, I was able to exert greater control by going beyond typical "allowable" theme customization using my own HTML, CSS and PHP code.

Staying with blue, orange and green palette from the MtnStuff logo, I chose to rely on rich "lifestyle" photography through the brand-related assets to which MtnStuff had access, both showing products in use and underscoring an outdoor, active look-and-feel.

Password-protected pages allow for retailers of specific brands to get targeted information for their product lines, and access order forms and documents as well as marketing and point-of-sale assets.

Content Management

While the client had well-formed ideas as to the site's objectives and how it should serve its users, they needed guidance as to what kind of content was needed to achieve those objectives.

Beginning with a thorough content inventory, I developed a content strategy and implemented collaboration tools, which allowed multiple of the site stakeholders to track what we had and what was still needed, with clear tasks and responsibilities assigned.

Finally, working within Wordpress' CMS platform, I developed a series of custom post types, in which documents, testimonials, team members, vendors and more could be easily added, modified and uploaded internally.

Results

Since the site relaunch, MtnStuff reports it has been getting considerable praise not only on the vast improvement of its online presence, but on the effectiveness of the site in better serving its retailer base. MtnStuff credits the site and its more professional exposure in part with its attraction of one, and soon to be two, additional clients.

The Redwood Group

  • Multilingual Site / UI Design
  • Visual Design
  • Content Management

The Redwood Group ("Redwood"), an industrial real estate investment trust (REIT) company with offices throughout Asia, and with whom I had previously worked to develop their brand and corporate identity, had asked me to redesign their corporate website to improve their online presence and better feature recent building projects.  

Site URL: www.redwoodgroup.com

The Challenge

The previous website was pretty static, was poorly managed and not regularly updated or translated. Redwood wanted a site that was both consistent with its own branding and which could quickly and easily accommodate updates for press releases, projects and other content. And there needed to be a Japanese and a Chinese version along with English.

Tha Approach

Discovery

I conducted stakeholder interviews and a content inventory of the existing site to get a sense of what kinds of improvements the client was envisioning. Having worked with Redwood previously in developing its brand and corporate identity, I already was well-grounded on the company culture and its business.

My background in Japanese and work experience in Asia enabled me to extend my contact with stakeholders, and take careful stock of cultural considerations for the site.

Defining the Requirements

Once I consolidated my findings, I was able to get buy-in from the client on the following site requirements:

  • Have a conservative but unique look and feel consistent with Redwood's corporate identity
  • Reinforce the logo and brand image
  • Adopt a culturally-sensitive color scheme
  • Accommodate regular updates in content
  • Be accessible and consistently updated in three languages
  • Be responsive

My Solution

Visual and Site Design

I wanted the landing page to emphasize of the corporate logo, which I had previously designed, and connect it with the strength of the redwood tree it represents. I created digital imaging effects, and animated the images with my own JavaScript.

For the main content pages, I created a monochrome site that supported the corporate identity and the "Redwood red", and I designed original icons for the site, using the same palette.

Site Structure and Layout

Coding the site to be completely responsive with HTML5, CSS3 and Javascript/jQuery, I employed Foundation as a grid framework, and compiled my stylesheets using Sass.

To accommodate large volumes of information on certain pages, I used a combination of accordion effects to show and hide content, along with modals to access more information on projects and executives, as well as to display full press releases.

I set up each page was linked to its version in the other languages, rather than lead the user back to a home page in the next language.

Content Management

It was absolutely crucial to create and maintain a solid content inventory, as practically every component of the site needed to be translated by a native translator, and then edited and checked for corporate tone by in-house staff, often requiring multiple rounds for specific pieces of content. Here my knowledge of Japanese and familiarity with Chinese proved to be an asset, as I assisted in the editing process.

Finally, used PHP to make information on individual projects and press releases accessible and easily updatable.

Chris Winslow Studio

  • Visual Design
  • Responsive Web Design
  • Front-end Development
  • Content Database Management

I created a thematic gallery site for mixed-media artist Chris Winslow, aimed at providing the site visitor with an immersive experience celebrating the color and movement the artist embraces.  

Site URL: www.chriswinslowstudio.com

The Challenge

After looking at multiple gallery templates and themes with DIY site builders, artist Chris Winslow asked me if I could help him display his works through an online presence that provided much more of a visual experience of who he is as an artist, than with a simple display of this work.

Additionally, he wanted to be able to provide information with each specific work for potential buyers, as they would appear in a physical gallery.

The Approach

After going through multiple gallery and portfolio sites with the artist, and learn what was lacking for him, I familiarized myself with his works, and the four categories/themes that contained them, to get a better sense of how we might approach displaying his work in a unique and representative way.

Defining the Requirements

The site needed to:

  • Present the artist's vision
  • Be evocative of the color and movement his art represents
  • Display more than 60 works
  • Display those works in the context of one of four categories
  • Include specific information on title, medium, size, year finished, availability and price on an accompanying ticket for each piece

My Solution

I proposed a site which, like a physical gallery, could be divided into thematic, open rooms that the site visitor could easily pass through, learn about the artist's approach and get detailed information on each individual piece.

Site Structure and Layout

Rather than create separate pages for each of these themes, I decided to treat the gallery itself as a single page, while each of these rooms could be represented through sliding panels which the visitor could easily open and enter.

Together with my own hand-coded HTML, I used JavaScript and jQuery to engage the visitor through the movement of the panels and the works they contain, while my CSS would display the sliding panels as either horizontal or vertical depending on the screen size for this responsive design.

Visual Design

I chose representative works of the artist to serve as backdrops for each of the themed panels, together with some of the vivid colors used by the artist, to heighten the sense of immersion for the site visitor.

Once within each themed space, I wanted to emulate the sense of being within a physical gallery space itself, where a visitor first takes in the collection, then when approaching each individual piece learns the title and, upon further inspection gets the accompanying information on each piece.

The challenge here was that the artist needed a means to display a "ticket" along with each full image containing all the specific information for the piece (including medium, size, year, availability and price of each work).

I determined the best way to achieve this was through individual modals to present the ticket information together with the enlarged image of each work, giving the user and prospective buyer an opportunity to get all pertinent information together.

Content Management

The need to create and reuse HTML to include more than 60 different works, as well as to keep the code both organized and easily editable was what led me to conclude that using PHP scripting and integrating a MySQL database that I could create and query was going to be the most efficient approach.

The result was a site that became very easily updatable. At any time the information on any piece changed, or when new pieces were added, the site could reflect those changes in minutes.

The client has been extremely happy with the site, which has increased his publicity and exposure, while giving his work greater accessibility overall.
